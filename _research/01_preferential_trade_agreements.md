abstract: |
  Preferential Trade Agreements (PTAs) are generally understood to promote trade by granting member countries preferential tariffs relative to third countries. We show that this assumption can break down during implementation. When PTAs phase out tariffs gradually, unilateral most-favored nation (MFN) tariff reductions can undercut negotiated preferential rates. We find that half of the 356 PTAs notified to the WTO do not adjust preferential rates following unilateral MFN reductions, making such reversals potentially common. Using Colombian transaction-level data from the U.S.–Colombia PTA, we show that 90\% of products subject to gradual phase-out had MFN rates below their original base rates even before the agreement entered into force, and that 35\% of imports expected to benefit did not effectively receive a preference in the early years. A triple-difference analysis shows that affected U.S. imports underperformed unaffected U.S. imports by 34\%, relative to imports from third countries. This effect persists even after preference margins are restored.
